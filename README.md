# NASDAQ-
Reason or explanation of high price movement in NASDAQ 
What it is

NASDAQ (National Association of Securities Dealers Automated Quotations) is the second-largest stock exchange in the world by market capitalization, after the NYSE. It launched in 1971 as the world's first electronic stock market — no physical trading floor, just a network of dealers making markets electronically. It's now owned and operated by Nasdaq, Inc. (itself a publicly traded company, ticker NDAQ).

Key indices
Nasdaq Composite (COMP) — tracks essentially every common stock listed on Nasdaq, over 2,500–3,000+ companies. It's market-cap weighted and heavily tilted toward tech because that's who lists there.
Nasdaq-100 (NDX) — the 100 largest non-financial companies on Nasdaq. This is the one tracked by the popular ETF QQQ. Heavily weighted toward mega-cap tech: Apple, Microsoft, Nvidia, Amazon, Alphabet, Meta, Broadcom, Tesla, etc.
What kind of companies list there

Nasdaq is known for technology, biotech, and growth companies — Apple, Microsoft, Amazon, Meta, Alphabet, Nvidia, Tesla, Intel, and most major biotech firms are Nasdaq-listed. It has less-strict initial listing requirements than the NYSE in some respects, which historically made it attractive to younger, high-growth companies (though it also has stricter tiers like the Nasdaq Global Select Market for larger, more established firms).

Trading mechanics
Trading hours: 9:30 AM–4:00 PM ET, with pre-market (4:00–9:30 AM) and after-hours (4:00–8:00 PM) sessions.
It operates as a dealer/market-maker system historically (distinct from the NYSE's specialist/auction model), though both exchanges now use largely electronic matching.
A few other things worth knowing
IPO hub: Nasdaq competes hard with NYSE for IPO listings and has landed a large share of major tech IPOs.
NDAQ stock: Nasdaq, Inc. itself is a for-profit company that also sells market data, listing services, and trading technology to other exchanges — that's a separate investment from "the Nasdaq index."
Volatility: Because it's tech-heavy, the Composite and Nasdaq-100 tend to be more volatile than the S&P 500 or Dow — bigger rallies in growth phases, sharper drawdowns when rates rise or tech sentiment sours.
The Nasdaq indices move based on a mix of macro forces and factors specific to the tech/growth stocks that dominate them. Here's what drives it:

Interest rates — probably the biggest lever

Nasdaq is packed with growth and tech companies, whose valuations lean heavily on future earnings. Higher interest rates make those future earnings worth less in today's dollars (discounted cash flow math), so the Nasdaq tends to be more sensitive to Fed policy than the Dow or S&P 500. Rate hikes or hawkish Fed commentary → Nasdaq often falls harder. Rate cuts or dovish signals → Nasdaq often rallies harder.

Earnings from mega-cap tech

Because the Nasdaq-100 is so top-heavy (Apple, Microsoft, Nvidia, Amazon, Alphabet, Meta, Broadcom, Tesla make up a huge chunk of the weighting), earnings reports and guidance from a handful of companies can move the whole index. Nvidia earnings day, for example, can swing the entire Nasdaq regardless of what's happening elsewhere in the economy.

AI and tech sentiment cycles

Since 2023 in particular, AI capex spending, chip demand, and data center buildout news have become major swing factors — moves in Nvidia, AMD, or cloud providers' AI spending plans ripple through the whole index.

Broader macro data
Inflation reports (CPI/PCE) — because they shape rate expectations
Jobs reports — strong jobs data can be read as "good for economy" or "bad because it delays rate cuts," so reactions vary
GDP growth, consumer spending data
Geopolitical events, trade policy, tariffs — tech supply chains (chips especially) are globally exposed
Currency and international exposure

Many Nasdaq giants earn large shares of revenue overseas, so a strong dollar can dent reported earnings, and vice versa.

Sector-specific shocks
Chip export controls or trade restrictions (especially US-China)
Regulatory action — antitrust cases against big tech, AI regulation
Supply chain disruptions (semiconductors, rare earths)
Market structure/technical factors
Index rebalancing (Nasdaq-100 does an annual and sometimes special reconstitution)
Options expiration and large hedging flows, since mega-caps have huge options markets
Passive fund flows — so much money is in QQQ and similar ETFs that flows in/out of those funds themselves can move prices
how a Fed decision actually turns into a Nasdaq price move, step by step:

The valuation math

Stock prices (roughly) reflect the present value of all a company's future cash flows, discounted back to today. The discount rate they use is heavily tied to interest rates — specifically the "risk-free rate" (Treasury yields) plus a risk premium.

The formula intuition: Value = Future Cash Flow ÷ (1 + discount rate)^years

Nasdaq companies — think Nvidia, Amazon, a biotech with no current profits — are priced heavily on cash flows years out, not next quarter. Compare that to a utility or bank stock, where more of the value comes from cash generated soon.

Why that makes Nasdaq rate-sensitive

Because Nasdaq's cash flows are further in the future, that denominator (1 + rate)^years compounds more. A 1-point rate change does much more damage the further out you go:

A cash flow 1 year out, discounted at 5% vs 4%: barely moves
A cash flow 15 years out, discounted at 5% vs 4%: moves a lot more

So when the Fed raises rates, long-duration growth stocks take a bigger valuation hit than short-duration value stocks — even if nothing about the business changed.

The full transmission chain
Fed signals rate move (meeting, speech, dot plot, inflation data surprise)
Treasury yields react first — the 10-year yield is the most-watched proxy
Discount rates get repriced across the market, hitting long-duration growth stocks hardest
Nasdaq-heavy names reprice down (or up) disproportionately vs. the Dow/S&P
Algorithmic and options flows amplify it — a lot of systematic trading is keyed directly to rate moves and volatility, so the initial move often overshoots
Follow-through in subsequent days as analysts revise price targets and earnings models using the new discount rate
Why "good news" can be "bad news" here

This is the classic Nasdaq quirk: a strong jobs report is good for the economy, but if it makes the Fed less likely to cut rates, Nasdaq can actually fall on strong economic data — because the rate-cut delay hurts growth stock valuations more than the strong economy helps.
Here's what's actually been moving the Nasdaq lately:

The big one: the Fed just hiked, unexpectedly

In September 2026 the Fed raised its target rate by a quarter point to 3.75%–4.00% — its first rate hike since 2023, with officials citing elevated inflation and aiming for a more timely return to the 2% target. That's a reversal from where markets thought things were headed (many had been pricing in cuts), and most Fed officials see at least one more hike later this year.

Fed Chair Warsh has been hawkish

Fed Chair Kevin Warsh's public remarks have repeatedly rattled the market. At the Jackson Hole symposium, Warsh conveyed worry over inflation trends, noting that summer inflation readings didn't show underlying trends had meaningfully improved, and that hawkish tone pushed the Nasdaq down on the day. This is a textbook case of the mechanism I described earlier — rate-sensitive tech stocks reacting hard to Fed tone shifts. 


Individual big-tech and chip moves swing the whole index

Because the Nasdaq-100 is so top-heavy, single-stock moves matter a lot:

A Nvidia rally of nearly 9% in one session helped lift the S&P 500 and Nasdaq to highs, before Nvidia and Intel shares fell over 3% the next day and dragged the Nasdaq down with them. 
More recently, gains have been led by names like Microsoft, with Microsoft rising after announcing plans to merge its consumer and workplace Copilot AI assistants into one product for corporate customers, while AI-related stocks have traded mixed — hardware names like Applied Materials and Lam Research rose while other parts of the group lagged. 

Jobs data whipsawing rate expectations

An unexpectedly strong August jobs report — payrolls grew 162,000 versus the roughly 53,000 economists expected — nudged up the odds of a Fed hike and hit stocks, illustrating the "good economic news, bad for Nasdaq" dynamic since it reduces the chance of easier policy. 

Oil and geopolitics feeding into yields

Falling oil prices have helped halt a surge in Treasury yields, with crude dropping on news that the US and Iran moved closer to easing tensions around Persian Gulf tanker blockades — lower yields take pressure off growth-stock valuations.
